\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Teleoperation cuts off the tactile signals that inform grip force in direct
grasping, so an operator must rely solely on vision to determine how hard a
remote gripper is squeezing. This becomes a problem with objects that show no
visual signs before breaking. This thesis implements tactile feedback to
complete the closed-loop haptic process and investigates whether tactile
feedback improves grasping of such objects, and whether the haptic actuator
used to deliver it matters.

The operator's thumb and index finger are tracked with markerless hand
tracking, and the resulting pinch aperture commands a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive
gripper equipped with vision-based 9DTact tactile sensors at its fingertips.
Contact deformation is reduced in real time to a deformation-volume force
proxy and a normalised haptic intensity value, which is streamed back to a
wearable actuator array on the operator's hand, driven by an ESP32-C6. The
same pipeline renders two mechanically distinct actuator types: linear
resonant actuators (LRAs), driven by a bipolar resonant carrier whose envelope
encodes intensity, and electromagnetic (EM) actuators, driven by short
single-direction pulses that pull a magnet against the fingerpad and release it
to return passively.

Twenty-two participants took part in a study comparing three feedback
conditions (visual-only, LRA, and EM) while grasping fragile and deformable
objects. For fragile objects, both types of tactile feedback raised the
object's survival rate significantly, from 62\% with vision alone to 81\% with
the LRA and 78\% with the EM, and the EM also cut the number of grip
adjustments the operator made after the grasp was complete. Squeezing after the
display had saturated fell by close to an order of magnitude, though that
reduction does not reach significance at this sample size. Deformable objects,
which have no clear breaking point, showed no difference between conditions.
Participants also preferred both haptic conditions over vision alone and most
often chose the LRA as their favourite, though no survey item and no objective
metric reliably told the two actuators apart.

These results show that tactile feedback clearly helps, but they do not show
which actuator helps more. A larger study, with counterbalanced condition
order, matched actuator placement, and a properly calibrated force
measurement, would be needed to rank the two actuator types with confidence.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

When a person picks up an egg, their fingers tell them how hard they are
squeezing, and they ease off before it cracks. That signal is missing in
\emph{teleoperation}, where an operator works a robot from a distance and never
touches the object at all.

Teleoperation is used wherever a person should not or cannot be present, such
as maintenance in nuclear and chemical plants, disposal of explosive ordnance,
search and rescue after disasters, and minimally invasive surgery
\cite{niemeyer2016}. A system of this kind has two halves. The \emph{local
    site} holds the operator and the equipment they use to see and command, while
the \emph{remote site} holds the robot, its sensors, and the objects being
handled \cite{niemeyer2016}. How much the operator decides varies. Under
\emph{direct control} they set the robot's motion themselves, and under
\emph{supervisory control} they give a goal and the robot works out how to
reach it \cite{niemeyer2016}. This thesis stays at the direct end, where the
operator's own hand motion is the command, so what the system sends back to
them sets the limit on what they can safely do.

What the system sends back is mostly video. A camera shows where the gripper is
and usually whether it has closed on the object, but an image says little about
the contact itself. This is a long-standing observation rather than a new one.
Howe and Cutkosky argued that successful manipulation needs to know contact
shape and contact force, and that these can only be measured through touch,
since people use touch to learn mechanical properties of the world that vision
cannot sense at all \cite{howe1992}. Recent teleoperation work reports the same
difficulty, noting that grip force and surface texture are both hard to read
from a picture \cite{becker2024}.

The operator is therefore left to set grip force without the sense that
normally sets it. For sturdy objects this hardly matters. It matters for two
classes of object that this thesis takes as its test cases. \emph{Fragile}
objects break suddenly once squeezed past a threshold, and \emph{deformable}
objects change shape gradually and never break at all. In both cases vision
gives the operator little warning, because the sign of damage either arrives
too late to act on or never appears.

\section{The Role of Touch in Human Grasping}
\label{sec:role-of-touch}

Gripping something carefully is not one action but a control loop that runs
continuously. It is fed by roughly 17\,000 touch receptors spread across the
grasping surfaces of the hand, spaced about \SI{0.7}{\milli\metre} apart at the
fingertip \cite{howe1992}. Four kinds of receptor share the work, and they
divide it by speed. Two respond only while the skin is moving, the FA-I
receptors over roughly \SIrange{5}{50}{\hertz} and the FA-II receptors over
roughly \SIrange{40}{400}{\hertz}. The other two report pressure that is not
changing, SA-I below about \SI{5}{\hertz} and SA-II for sustained force and
skin tension \cite{johansson2009}. This split matters here because an
artificial device does not stimulate touch in general. It stimulates whichever
of these receptors its own motion happens to reach.

The loop these receptors feed works mainly by prediction. A person sets grip
force in advance from what they expect the object's weight and surface to be,
and the result is efficient, with grip force normally only
\SIrange{10}{40}{\percent} above the minimum needed to stop the object slipping
\cite{johansson2009}. That economy holds even when weight and surface friction
vary widely \cite{howe1992}. When the prediction is wrong, touch does trigger a
correction, but not immediately. The delay around these corrective loops is
roughly \SI{100}{\milli\second}, which is why skilled handling relies on
predicting well rather than on reacting fast \cite{johansson2009}. Take the
touch signal away and the ability to match grip to the surface is lost, as
happens when sensation in the fingers is impaired \cite{johansson2009}.

Two points follow for anything built to replace that signal. First, much of
what matters arrives as separate events rather than as a running force reading.
Recordings from single nerve fibres show the FA-I receptors signalling the
earliest stage of slip and triggering a reflexive, unconscious increase in grip
force, while the FA-II receptors report the making and breaking of contact
\cite{howe1992}. Second, the loop never stops running, so a useful artificial
display should be quick and always available rather than a one-off alarm that
fires when some threshold is crossed.

\section{Delivering Touch Back to the Operator}
\label{sec:existing-approaches}

It helps to separate two senses that touch combines. \emph{Cutaneous} sensing
responds to stimuli applied to the skin, \emph{kinaesthetic} sensing reports
the position and motion of the limbs together with the forces the muscles
exert, and using both at once to learn about the world is called \emph{haptic}
perception \cite{howe1992}. Wearable devices are built against the same
division. Kinaesthetic devices such as hand exoskeletons brace against the
world or the forearm and can resist the fingers directly, but moving that
bracing point closer to the skin makes a device easier to wear while steadily
removing its ability to resist motion, until only cutaneous cues remain
\cite{pacchierotti2017}. This thesis works at the cutaneous end, where the
display is small enough to wear without obstructing the hand.

Two families of cutaneous actuator are compared here, and they act on the skin
in different ways. A \emph{linear resonant actuator} (LRA) is a small vibrating
mass whose frequency is fixed by its mechanical resonance, typically near
\SI{200}{\hertz} \cite{choi2013}, which places it in the band the FA-II
receptors respond to \cite{johansson2009}. An \emph{electromagnetic} (EM)
actuator instead pulls a magnet against the fingerpad on each pulse, giving a
discrete contact event rather than a continuous vibration
\cite{johansson2009,vechev2019}. Designs in this family can in principle be
built to latch and hold a steady pressure the fast receptors do not register at
all; the unit built for this thesis was verified on the bench not to do so, and
Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive} returns to what that means for the comparison.
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} develops this contrast and the evidence behind it.

One limit applies to either family. A cutaneous display carries intensity
coarsely, since an amplitude difference of at least \SIrange{20}{30}{\percent}
is needed before people can tell two vibration levels apart reliably
\cite{choi2013}. Such a display can convey a rough sense of how hard the grip
is tightening, but not a force reading, and that bounds what any system of this
type can be expected to do.

\section{Tactile Sensing on the Robot Side}
\label{sec:tactile-sensing}

The other half of the problem is what the robot can measure. A tactile array
has to recover either the shape of the contact or the pressure spread across
it, and recovering shape calls for a soft skin \cite{cutkosky2016,howe1992}.
Vision-based sensors do this by reading a soft skin with a camera rather than
with a grid of wired elements, which is what made compact fingertip sensing
practical \cite{li2024}. The 9DTact sensor used here works out a depth map of
the contact from image brightness alone, reports a mean error of
\SI{0.046}{\milli\metre} against known shapes, measures
$32.5 \times 25.5 \times 25.5$~\si{\milli\metre}, and runs at up to 90 frames
per second \cite{lin2023}. It is small enough to replace the fingertip pad on
an ordinary two-finger gripper without redesigning the finger.

This leaves a mismatch that any system like this has to settle openly. The
sensor produces a full depth map for each finger, while the display on the
operator's hand can carry only about one coarse number per channel. Getting
from one to the other means deliberately discarding information, and how that
is done is a design choice in its own right. A comparison between actuators is
meaningless unless that choice is held fixed. This thesis states the reduction
explicitly in Section~\ref{subsec:mapping} and applies exactly the same one in
both haptic conditions.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

One point of terminology is worth settling before the questions are stated. The
system here is a direct-control system in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:background}, but it is not \emph{bilateral}, a term reserved
in the telerobotics literature for systems that push force back at the operator
through the very mechanism that measures their motion \cite{niemeyer2016}. In
this system the command travels one way only. A camera tracks the gap between
the operator's thumb and index finger and maps it to gripper position, with no
mechanism for the robot to push back against. What returns to the operator is a
separate cutaneous display. The question is how much of ordinary grip control
such a display can restore.

This thesis therefore asks a two-part question that the literature has usually
split up:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Does touch feedback, taken from a real vision-based tactile sensor
          and delivered through a wearable actuator array, measurably improve
          grip-force control and grasp outcomes when an operator handles
          fragile and deformable objects, compared with vision alone?
    \item Given the same sensor and the same intensity calculation, does the
          \emph{type} of actuator that delivers the signal change those
          outcomes?
\end{enumerate}

The second question is the one the work is built to answer, because answering
it requires a system in which everything before the actuator can be held
identical while the actuator itself is swapped.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Integrate vision-based tactile sensing into a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive
          gripper and derive a real-time contact-intensity signal from it.
    \item Design and build a five-channel wearable actuator driver, based on an
          ESP32-C6, that can deliver that signal through either LRA or EM
          pulsed actuators with no change anywhere upstream of the
          driver. Two channels are used in this study, one per instrumented
          gripper finger.
    \item Command the gripper from the operator's own pinch aperture using
          markerless hand tracking, so that the effort of controlling the
          gripper is comparable across conditions.
    \item Run a within-subjects user study comparing visual-only, LRA, and EM
          feedback on fragile and deformable objects, collecting both per-tick
          quantitative logs and per-condition subjective ratings.
    \item Analyse the resulting data to answer the two questions of
          Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}, and state plainly which
          confounds limit how firmly they can be answered.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis contributes the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A complete teleoperation stack covering tactile sensing, hand
          tracking, gripper control, the serial link to the hand, and the
          firmware, in which the actuator is the only part that changes between
          conditions.
    \item A way of rendering graded intensity on electromagnetic actuators,
          which each pulse delivers as a single discrete contact event rather
          than a continuous sensation, by pulsing them in bursts and gaps.
          This makes a like-for-like comparison against a continuously
          modulated LRA possible.
    \item A within-subjects study with $N=22$ participants measuring the effect
          of touch feedback, and of actuator type, on grip-force control and
          object survival for fragile and deformable objects. The sample is
          larger than those of the closest comparable studies, which report
          seven to ten participants \cite{becker2024,coppola2022}, and was
          sized for the harder comparison between the two actuators rather than
          the easier one against no feedback.
    \item A metric, \emph{excess deformation}, that isolates the squeezing an
          operator does after the display has already reached full intensity,
          when further squeezing tells them nothing new. The metric is defined
          against the mapping in Section~\ref{subsec:mapping}.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}
% =====================================================================

This chapter reviews the four bodies of work the thesis draws on. The first is
teleoperation and the problem of returning contact information to the operator.
The second is tactile sensing on the robot, and in particular the vision-based
sensors that make fingertip measurement practical. The third is the wearable
haptic hardware that delivers a signal to the skin, together with the evidence
that doing so helps. The fourth narrows to the two actuator families this study
compares. A final section states the gap that follows.

\section{Teleoperation and the Feedback Problem}
\label{sec:lit-grasping}

Telerobotic control architectures are usually placed on a spectrum running from
direct control, where the operator specifies motion and the robot supplies
none, through shared control, to supervisory control, where the robot holds
enough autonomy to refine a high-level goal \cite{niemeyer2016}. Systems that
also return force to the operator through the master mechanism are called
bilateral, and they have attracted most of the field's attention because they
promise telepresence \cite{niemeyer2016}. They also carry the field's hardest
control problems, since closing a force loop across two sites raises stability
questions that are normally handled by requiring the interconnection to be
passive \cite{niemeyer2016}.

A bilateral architecture is not the only way to return contact information, and
for a lightweight system it is not always the practical one. The alternative
taken here keeps the command unilateral and adds a separate sensory channel.
The design question then moves from stability to information, namely what
should be measured at the remote site and what should be shown to the operator.

That question has received less attention than the command path. Zheng et al.\
make the point directly in a study of teleoperated manufacturing, observing
that most work concentrates on getting commands to the robot and comparatively
little on the channel running back, which they identify as the one that governs
operator cognitive load \cite{zheng2024}. Their own system grasps fragile
cylindrical products, a task chosen precisely because mishandling is
unrecoverable \cite{zheng2024}.

Where richer feedback is offered, it is often computed guidance rather than
measurement. Huang et al.\ compared haptic guidance virtual fixtures against
two- and three-dimensional visualisation in a telemanipulation task, and found
that the virtual fixture gave significantly better collision avoidance than
improved visualisation alone \cite{huang2019}. A virtual fixture constrains the
operator according to a model of the task, so it helps most when the task is
known in advance. It does not report what the gripper is touching, which is the
quantity this thesis is concerned with.

The underlying reason contact must be measured rather than inferred was set out
long before these systems existed. Howe and Cutkosky argued that manipulation
requires knowledge of contact shape and contact force, that these can only be
obtained through touch, and that people rely on touch for mechanical properties
vision cannot sense \cite{howe1992}. The same argument appears in the modern
robotics literature, where grasp force control, contact location and grasp
stability are listed as the manipulation tasks tactile sensing serves
\cite{cutkosky2016}.

\section{Vision-Based Tactile Sensing}
\label{sec:lit-vbts}

A tactile array has to recover either the shape of the contact or the pressure
distributed across it, and recovering shape requires a compliant skin over the
sensing elements \cite{cutkosky2016,howe1992}. For a long time this was easier
to state than to build. Howe and Cutkosky identified packaging as the limiting
problem, naming both the difficulty of making an array small and curved enough
to sit on a robot finger and the need to reduce the number of wires running to
it \cite{howe1992}. They added a placement constraint, since the finer the
task, the closer the sensor must sit to the contact so that the compliance and
inertia of the intervening structure do not corrupt the measurement
\cite{howe1992}. Writing in 1992 they could report no use of tactile sensing in
the real-time control of manipulation at all, and judged the remaining
obstacles to be ones of integration rather than of transducer design
\cite{howe1992}.

Vision-based sensors, also called visuotactile sensors, addressed the wiring
and packaging problem by replacing the wired grid with a camera. Such a sensor
has three parts, a soft sensing skin that takes the contact, a lighting system
matched to that skin, and a camera that images how the skin deforms
\cite{li2024}. Because the output is an image, resolution costs pixels rather
than wires, and the quantities derived from the image stream are computed in
software. A recent review groups these into contact-area segmentation,
three-dimensional reconstruction of the contact geometry, force estimation,
slip detection, and localisation \cite{li2024}.

Slip is the quantity that connects most directly to grasping, and it is also
the one where tactile sensing has least competition from vision. The robotics
literature defines \emph{incipient slip} as the small localised vibrations that
appear around the edge of a contact patch before the object begins to move, and
detecting them early is what allows a grasp to be corrected before it fails
\cite{cutkosky2016}. Visuotactile sensors support this and more. Jawale et al.\
extract deformation-field features from a GelSight sensor and train one model
to detect slip and a second to estimate its severity, reporting \SI{92}{\percent}
detection accuracy and using the severity estimate inside a feedback loop so
that corrections are proportionate rather than maximal \cite{jawale2024}. That
work closes the loop around the robot's own controller rather than around a
human, which is the distinction this thesis turns on.

The sensor used here belongs to this family. 9DTact reconstructs a depth map of
the contact from image brightness alone, without markers printed on the gel,
and reports a mean absolute error of \SI{0.046}{\milli\metre} against known
geometry \cite{lin2023}. It measures
$32.5 \times 25.5 \times 25.5$~\si{\milli\metre}, weighs \SI{20}{\gram}, and
runs at up to 90 frames per second \cite{lin2023}. Those figures matter for the
present application less because of their absolute accuracy than because they
fit inside a commercial gripper finger while leaving the control loop fast
enough to drive a haptic display.

\section{Wearable Haptic Feedback in Teleoperation}
\label{sec:lit-haptics}

Wearable haptic interfaces are conventionally organised by where the device is
grounded. Grounded and body-grounded kinaesthetic interfaces can resist finger
motion directly, and as the grounding point moves toward the skin the device
becomes more wearable while progressively losing that ability, until only
cutaneous cues remain \cite{pacchierotti2017}. Pacchierotti et al.\ argue that
this trade is often worth making, since the density and low activation
threshold of skin receptors allow cutaneous displays to be compact,
comfortable, and inexpensive, and cutaneous feedback has repeatedly improved
performance in teleoperation and immersive systems \cite{pacchierotti2017}.

The empirical case for adding such feedback is reasonably strong, although the
studies differ in setting and none isolates the actuator. Coppola et al.\ built
an affordable system combining Leap Motion hand tracking with a custom
vibrotactile glove, and found with ten participants that feedback improved
grasping performance and significantly reduced reported effort and physical
demand, though their robot hand was simulated \cite{coppola2022}. Gong et al.\
took the opposite approach to fidelity, measuring tool vibrations with
accelerometers and playing a scaled version back through voice-coil actuators
during telerobotic assembly with a da Vinci system. With thirty participants
they measured significantly lower vibrations and contact forces once operators
had gained some experience with the feedback, together with subjective reports
of greater realism and lower perceived difficulty \cite{gong2024}.

Two results bear directly on the object classes used here. Chai et al.\ encoded
grip force as electrotactile stimulation for users of a myoelectric hand and
tested it with a virtual eggs task, which measures whether a user can grasp
without breaking. Feedback improved fragile-object handling across four
different encoding strategies, and also allowed users to discriminate four
levels of object stiffness at better than \SI{86}{\percent} accuracy
\cite{chai2022}. The setting is prosthetics rather than teleoperation, so the
result supports the narrower claim that encoded contact information helps with
fragile objects, not the broader one about teleoperated systems.

Closer still is the work of Becker et al., who mounted GelSight sensors on a
robot gripper, estimated contact force from marker motion and from a neural
network, and returned the result as vibration through commercial haptic gloves
inside a virtual-reality teleoperation pipeline \cite{becker2024}. In a
preliminary study with seven participants asked to pick up a plasticine ball as
gently as possible, deformation of the ball fell by roughly
\SI{48}{\percent} when haptic feedback was enabled \cite{becker2024}. This is
the closest published precedent for the system built here. It compares feedback
against no feedback using a single actuator, and its authors describe it as
preliminary.

On the hardware side, the practical objection to placing several actuators on a
hand has weakened. Luo et al.\ used digital embroidery to fabricate tactile
sensors and arrays of vibrotactile actuators directly into textiles, and
demonstrated the resulting gloves recording, reproducing, and transferring
tactile interactions, including in robot teleoperation \cite{luo2024}. Their
machine-learning pipeline also adapts feedback parameters to individual users,
which is a reminder that perceived intensity is not uniform across people
\cite{luo2024}.

\section{Two Families of Wearable Actuator}
\label{sec:lit-actuators}

The choice of actuator is not perceptually neutral, and the wearable haptics
literature states the relevant trade explicitly. Resonant-mass actuators are
simple and efficient but cannot change their vibration frequency at all, while
solenoid and voice-coil designs can follow an arbitrary drive signal within
their limits and can additionally hold a steady force, which a resonant mass
cannot \cite{pacchierotti2017}. The two families compared in this thesis sit on
either side of that statement.

\subsection{Linear Resonant Actuators}
\label{subsec:lit-lra}

A linear resonant actuator is a mass suspended on a spring and driven
electromagnetically, so its useful output is confined to a narrow band around
its mechanical resonance. Commercially available units are on the order of
\SI{10}{\milli\metre} across with resonant frequencies near \SI{200}{\hertz}
\cite{choi2013}. That frequency falls inside the range the FA-II receptors
respond to \cite{johansson2009}, which is consistent with the general finding
that vibrotactile perception in hairless skin is governed by the rapidly
adapting and Pacinian channels \cite{choi2013}.

Two consequences follow for rendering. First, intensity must be varied by
changing drive amplitude or by gating the carrier, because frequency is not
available as a control variable. Second, the resolution of whatever scale
results is coarse. Weber fractions for vibration intensity cluster around
\SIrange{10}{30}{\percent}, and Choi and Kuchenbecker report that in practice a
difference of at least \SIrange{20}{30}{\percent} in amplitude is needed for
reliable discrimination \cite{choi2013}. A vibrotactile display therefore
supports a monotonic sense of magnitude rather than a metric one, which is the
constraint the excess-deformation metric of this thesis is built around.

\subsection{Electromagnetic Bistable Pin Actuators}
\label{subsec:lit-em}

The second family drives a pin into the fingerpad and holds it there by
magnetic latching, so power is drawn only while switching between states. Pece
et al.\ demonstrated the principle in a flexible wearable array, placing rigid
bistable actuation cells in a soft printed case and using magnetic shielding to
prevent neighbouring magnets interfering, which allowed a denser arrangement
than earlier designs. Their prototype achieved \SIrange{160}{200}{\milli\newton}
of latching force over \SI{2}{\milli\metre} of travel at a \SI{17}{\milli\metre}
pitch \cite{pece2017}.

Vechev et al.\ refined the same mechanism into the TacTiles design this thesis
adopts, halving the physical volume and cutting power consumption by a factor
of four while retaining \SI{200}{\milli\newton} of holding force, in a unit of
\SI{1}{\centi\metre\cubed} and \SI{1.8}{\gram} drawing \SI{130}{\milli\watt} on
average \cite{vechev2019}. The design supports two modes. In contact mode the
pin travels to the skin and rests against it, held by the bistable latch until
release is commanded, so the skin is stimulated directly. In pulse mode the pin
moves for only a few milliseconds and retracts, so the user feels the
actuator's recoil rather than the pin itself \cite{vechev2019}. Their argument
for the design is that vibration reaches only the high-frequency receptors and
so cannot convincingly render either a discrete touch event or the steady
sensation of contact \cite{vechev2019}. Ten participants distinguished the two
modes in \SI{87}{\percent} of trials and localised individual actuators in
\SI{88}{\percent}, so the distinction is one users can perceive
\cite{vechev2019}.

The relevance to grip-force rendering is that a latched pin delivers pressure
that does not change, and neither fast receptor type registers unchanging
force, leaving the slowly adapting SA-I and SA-II receptors to report it
\cite{johansson2009}. In principle this gives the two families access to
different parts of the sensory apparatus. The unit built for this thesis does
not realise contact mode at all: bench verification (a randomised, blinded
direction check, described in Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive}) found that only
one polarity of the drive pulse moves the actuator, so it cannot be commanded
to extend and hold; it can only be pulsed, and the actuator returns passively
once the pulse ends. The graded-intensity scheme therefore renders every EM
percept in what Vechev et al.\ call pulse mode, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to what that costs against the
contact-mode comparison this section describes.

\section{The Comparative Gap}
\label{sec:lit-gap}

Three observations follow from the work reviewed above.

The first is that tactile feedback helps. Across prosthetics, telerobotic
assembly, and visuotactile teleoperation, adding a cutaneous channel reduced
contact force, improved handling of fragile objects, or lowered reported effort
\cite{chai2022,gong2024,coppola2022,becker2024}.

The second is that no study isolates the actuator. Each of these pairs one
actuator with one sensing pipeline and one rendering scheme, so a measured
benefit belongs to the whole chain. Where two conditions are compared, the
sensing or control path usually differs alongside the actuator.

The third is that there is a mechanistic reason to expect actuator type to
matter. A resonant vibrator and a latching pin do not merely render the same
signal with different fidelity. They stimulate different receptor populations
and offer different rendering primitives, one a fixed-frequency carrier and the
other a discrete contact event \cite{choi2013,vechev2019,johansson2009}.

Taken together these leave a specific question unanswered, namely whether the
actuator alone changes grasp outcomes when the sensor, the intensity
calculation, and the control mapping are all held identical. This thesis is
built to supply that comparison.

\section{Overall Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

The system forms a single closed loop that begins and ends at the operator's
hand. The operator pinches, a camera tracks the pinch aperture, the aperture is
mapped to a commanded gripper opening, the gripper closes on the object, the
tactile sensors report the resulting contact deformation, the deformation is
reduced to a scalar intensity, and that intensity is rendered on the actuators
worn on the operator's fingertips. Figure~\ref{fig:signalflow} shows the signal
path and the boundary between the host PC and the wearable hardware.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{preprint_fig1.png}
    \caption{Signal flow of the teleoperation loop. Everything upstream of the
        haptic driver is identical in the LRA and EM conditions, and only the
        actuator and its drive stage differ.}
    \label{fig:signalflow}
\end{figure}

The host runs four cooperating threads, each on its own timer rather than on a
shared clock. Hand tracking runs at the hand camera's frame rate, gripper
commands are issued at \SI{25}{\hertz}, tactile reads and the haptic serial
send share a \SI{15}{\hertz} loop, and a status poll of the gripper runs at
\SI{10}{\hertz}. The haptic path is therefore the slowest stage in the chain,
with a new intensity value reaching the actuator roughly every
\SI{67}{\milli\second}. That figure is a sampling interval and not an
end-to-end latency, which remains unmeasured for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:latency}.

\section{Operator-Side Control: Hand Tracking}
\label{sec:handtracking}

The gripper is commanded from the operator's own hand rather than from a
joystick or slider, so that the motor act of closing the gripper resembles the
motor act of closing the hand. A single RGB camera at
$640 \times 480$ observes the operator's dominant hand, and a MediaPipe hand
landmarker running in video mode returns 2D landmark positions for one hand.
The pinch aperture $p$ is the pixel distance between the thumb tip and the
index tip, landmarks 4 and 8.

Two detector settings depart from the library defaults. Detection, presence and
tracking confidence thresholds are lowered to 0.4, 0.5 and 0.5 respectively,
because the actuator body worn on the thumb and index fingertips partly covers
the two landmarks the mapping depends on. At the stock thresholds this caused
the tracker to lose hand presence entirely during the EM condition.

Aperture is mapped to the gripper's commanded position register by linear
interpolation between two fixed pixel thresholds, a closed threshold
$p_{\text{min}} = \SI{40}{px}$ and an open threshold
$p_{\text{max}} = \SI{180}{px}$,
\begin{equation}
    q = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(
    \left(1 - \frac{p - p_{\text{min}}}{p_{\text{max}} - p_{\text{min}}}\right)
    q_{\text{max}},\; 0,\; q_{\text{max}}\right),
    \label{eq:pinchmap}
\end{equation}
where $q_{\text{max}} = 225$ counts is the fully closed gripper position. The
closed threshold is set above the bare-finger contact distance of roughly
\SI{30}{px} because the actuator module adds physical standoff between the two
fingertips.

Three filters sit between the raw landmark distance and the commanded position.
A \SI{1.5}{px} dead-band on the aperture suppresses sub-pixel landmark jitter.
An input gate commits a new target only when it differs from the last committed
target by more than 2 counts. The committed target is then smoothed by a
first-order exponential filter with coefficient $\alpha = 0.45$ applied per
frame, and the smoothed value snaps to the target once the two are within one
count, so the gripper settles rather than creeping. A further dead-band of 3
counts on the gripper output suppresses motor stutter while the hand is
stationary.

When the tracker loses the hand, the committed target is reset to the current
smoothed value rather than to zero, so the gripper holds its position instead
of releasing. The same hold applies when the operator pauses the session, which
is used during setup and during the firmware swap that a change of condition
requires.

One consequence of Equation~\eqref{eq:pinchmap} should be stated plainly. The
two thresholds are constants retuned per rig and per actuator thickness, not
per participant. Hand size and camera distance therefore vary between
participants without being compensated, so the same physical pinch does not map
to the same gripper position for everyone. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} takes
up what this costs.

\subsection{Calibration and Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:sensorcalib}

Calibration follows the 9DTact procedure \cite{lin2023}. A reference frame of
the undeformed gel is captured by averaging 30 frames, then a ball of known
radius is pressed into the gel at a set of positions to fit the mapping from
pixel intensity to indentation depth. Each incoming frame is converted to a
depth map of the contact patch by subtracting the reference and applying that
mapping. Pixels deforming by more than \SI{0.1}{\milli\metre} are counted as
contact. Two quantities are derived per frame.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deformation volume}, the integral of indentation depth over
          the contact region, in arbitrary units. This is the primary
          grip-force proxy used throughout the analysis. It is uncalibrated, so
          it is monotonic in applied force but is not expressed in newtons.
    \item \textbf{Maximum indentation depth}, the peak of the depth map, in
          millimetres. This is the quantity mapped to haptic intensity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Depth-to-Intensity Mapping}
\label{subsec:mapping}

Maximum indentation depth $d$ is mapped to a normalised haptic intensity
$u \in [0,1]$ by clipping and linear scaling against a fixed ceiling
$d_{\max}$,
\begin{equation}
    u = \min\!\left(\frac{\max(d - d_0,\, 0)}{d_{\max} - d_0},\; 1\right),
    \label{eq:mapping}
\end{equation}
where $d_0$ is the no-contact noise floor established at calibration and
$d_{\max} = \SI{1.0}{\milli\metre}$ is a safety ceiling. Reaching that ceiling
on either sensor also blocks further closing at the gripper, though never
further opening, which protects the sensor fixture from the excess torque a
rigid object would otherwise transmit. Equation~\eqref{eq:mapping} is identical
in both haptic conditions, and the conditions differ only in how $u$ is
realised physically.

The saturation implied by Equation~\eqref{eq:mapping} has a direct experimental
consequence. Once $d$ reaches $d_{\max}$ the display stops changing, so any
further squeezing delivers no new information to the operator. Deformation
volume accumulated after that point is, by construction, effort the feedback
channel could not have discouraged. This is the \emph{excess deformation}
metric defined in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}.

\section{ESP32-C6 Haptic Driver}
\label{sec:driver}

Intensity values are streamed from the host to an ESP32-C6 over a USB serial
link at \SI{115200}{baud}. Each packet is one ASCII line carrying the two
channel intensities to four decimal places, which the firmware clamps to
$[0,1]$ and discards if malformed, so a corrupted line cannot stop the drive
loop. The firmware holds the last commanded intensity and regenerates the drive
waveform locally, which decouples waveform timing from the
\SI{15}{\hertz} packet rate. A watchdog zeroes both channels if no packet
arrives for \SI{200}{\milli\second}, so a host failure silences the display
rather than leaving an actuator latched against the skin.

The driver board carries five channels, wired as five PWM and enable pairs for
the LRA path and five IN1/IN2 pin pairs for the EM path. This study drives two
of them, one on the thumb and one on the index finger, matching the two
instrumented gripper fingers. The remaining three are available for the spatial
rendering discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} as future work.

\subsection{LRA Drive}
\label{subsec:lra-drive}

Each linear resonant actuator is driven differentially with a bipolar
square-wave carrier at \SI{200}{\hertz}, produced by flipping the commanded
polarity every \SI{2500}{\micro\second}, on the assumption that an H-bridge
sits between the microcontroller pins and the motor. Intensity is rendered by
envelope modulation, so within a fixed \SI{50}{\milli\second} window the
carrier is gated on for a fraction of the window equal to $u$ and the drive
coasts for the remainder. The envelope therefore repeats at \SI{20}{\hertz}.
The LRA channels share their physical pins with the EM channels
(Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive}), on which bench verification found only one
polarity to be effective; that finding was not independently confirmed on the
LRA path, since a genuinely unipolar drive would still produce an audible tap
rather than silence, but it means the bipolar-carrier description above should
be read as the design intent rather than as a confirmed property of this
board.

This is a compromise rather than the ideal drive. An LRA's perceived amplitude
is properly modulated by varying the drive amplitude at the actuator's resonant
frequency. The microcontroller's standard PWM peripheral cannot generate the
phase-locked antiphase pair that amplitude-modulated bipolar drive would
require, so the on-fraction of a fixed-amplitude carrier stands in for
amplitude. The carrier frequency was also set from bench testing rather than
tuned to the measured resonance of these particular actuators. Both
consequences are taken up in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{EM Actuator Drive}
\label{subsec:em-drive}

Each electromagnetic actuator is built as a permanent magnet suspended between
two coil faces in a 3D-printed housing, following the general form of the
bistable design in \cite{vechev2019}, which is meant to latch magnetically in
either an extended or retracted state and require power only to switch between
them. The firmware assumes that design and drives each channel through an
IN1/IN2 pin pair, with one polarity intended to extend the pin against the
operator's fingerpad and the opposite polarity intended to retract it, using
\SI{6}{\milli\second} engage and \SI{10}{\milli\second} release pulses measured
on the bench.

This assumption was checked directly rather than taken on trust. A randomised,
blinded direction test (six pulses, order hidden until after every trial was
scored, repeated on both study channels) found that only one polarity moves
the actuator at all; the opposite polarity produces no sensation in either
direction, and the actuator returns passively once the effective pulse ends,
rather than being driven back. The unit built for this study is therefore not
bistable in practice: it is a single-direction pulsed electromagnet with a
passive mechanical return, whatever the intended design. The firmware still
issues both polarities every cycle, since the ineffective one is harmless, but
only the effective pulse does anything, and no configuration of this hardware
can hold a steady extended contact. This behaviour was verified with the hand
in its normal wearing orientation; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reports a
follow-up check suggesting the passive return, and possibly the pull itself,
may be gravity-assisted rather than a property of the actuator alone.

Because the actuator produces no ongoing sensation from a single pulse, a
sustained and graded percept is approximated by pulsing repeatedly. The
firmware emits bursts of ten
pulses, each \SI{4}{\milli\second} long, separated by
a gap that carries the intensity. The gap runs from \SI{400}{\milli\second} at
$u = 0$ down to \SI{35}{\milli\second} at $u = 1$, interpolated linearly, so
higher intensity produces more frequent contact events per unit time rather
than stronger ones. Channels are staggered by \SI{10}{\milli\second} to keep
two coils from switching on the same instant. Intensities below 0.1 are treated
as no contact.

The pin is therefore never held extended during a trial. The sustained-pressure
capability described in Section~\ref{sec:existing-approaches} is not exercised,
which narrows the intended contrast between the two actuators to one of
temporal granularity. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this.

Both actuators are carried in printed modules strapped to the thumb and index

between the fingertips, which interacts with the pinch mapping of

\section{Data Logging}
\label{sec:logging-arch}

The host writes one CSV row per haptic tick for the duration of each recorded
trial, containing the timestamp, commanded and measured gripper position, motor
current, deformation volume, maximum indentation depth, the rendered haptic
intensity for each channel, and the active condition and object class. Rows are
therefore written at \SI{15}{\hertz}. At the end of a fragile trial the operator
records whether the object survived, and every trial is explicitly kept or
discarded before the file is closed. These logs are the sole source of the
quantitative metrics in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Participants}
\label{sec:participants}

Twenty-two participants ($N=22$) took part in the study. Eligibility required
normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no diagnosed sensory or motor
impairment affecting the dominant hand. A screening item recorded any such
impairment so that affected responses could be excluded from the subjective
analysis. Age, gender, height and dominant hand were recorded, along with three
separate self-reports of prior experience, covering teleoperation, virtual
reality and gaming controllers, and the operation of robotic arms. Prior
experience was not required for eligibility. All participants gave informed
consent before taking part.

The sample is larger than those of the closest comparable studies, which report
seven to ten participants \cite{becker2024,coppola2022}. The larger sample was
recruited to improve power for the actuator-versus-actuator comparison, which
was expected in advance to show a smaller effect than the comparison between
feedback and no feedback.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

The study uses a within-subjects design with one factor, feedback condition, at
three levels, namely \textbf{visual-only} (V), \textbf{LRA}, and \textbf{EM}.
Every participant experienced all three, which controls for individual
differences in manual dexterity and grip-force perception that would otherwise
dominate a between-subjects comparison at this sample size.

Conditions were presented in the fixed order visual-only, then LRA, then EM.
They were not counterbalanced. Each condition requires its own firmware on the
ESP32-C6, and reflashing the board mid-session takes long enough that a
randomised order was judged impractical during the session design. The cost is
that practice and fatigue are confounded with condition. Any effect attributed
to an actuator may in part reflect its position in the session, and this is
carried into Section~\ref{sec:limitations} rather than treated as controlled.

Within each condition, participants performed five trials on each object class.
That gives ten trials per condition, thirty trials per participant, and 110
trials per condition per object class across the sample, for 660 analysed
trials in total.

A small number of participants also ran exploratory conditions that are not
part of the analysis. Seven ran a variant in which the electromagnetic pin was
driven as a single latching contact rather than as a pulse train, and two ran
variants of the LRA and EM conditions with the video feed removed. These
recordings are retained in the dataset but excluded from every result reported
in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, since neither variant was run on enough
participants to support a within-subjects test.

\section{Task and Materials}
\label{sec:task}

Participants sat at a workstation facing a display carrying the remote-view
camera feed, with no direct line of sight to the gripper. In the LRA and EM
conditions they wore the corresponding actuator modules on the thumb and index
finger of the dominant hand. The gripper stood vertically with its fingers
pointing upward, and each object was presented in a fixed cradle at the end of
a horizontal support arm, positioned so that the object centre sat at a
constant height of \SI{16.5}{\centi\metre} aligned with the centre of the open
finger gap. The gripper body did not move during a trial, and only the fingers
opened and closed under hand-tracking control.

Each trial consisted of closing the gripper on the presented object, holding
briefly, and releasing it without dropping or visibly damaging it. Two object
classes were used, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:objects}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fragile}, a hollow two-part plastic egg, which separates
          abruptly once the applied force exceeds the seam strength. Failure is
          discrete and unambiguous, and the object is reassembled or replaced
          between trials.
    \item \textbf{Deformable}, a foam cube, which yields progressively and
          recovers between trials, with no discrete failure point.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{preprint_fig4.png}
    \caption{Object classes. (a) The hollow plastic egg used as the fragile
        object. (b) The same egg with its halves separated, the failure event scored
        as breakage. (c) The foam cube used as the deformable object.}
    \label{fig:objects}
\end{figure}

The two object classes are not matched on difficulty and are not intended to
be. The fragile object carries a failure threshold that the feedback channel
could plausibly help an operator avoid, while the deformable object carries
none. The deformable class therefore acts as a control, since a channel whose
value lies in discouraging over-squeezing has nothing to act on when
over-squeezing costs nothing.

\section{Session Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Figure~\ref{fig:methodflow} summarises the session. Each session began with
informed consent and a task explanation, followed by a check that the pinch
mapping of Equation~\eqref{eq:pinchmap} produced usable travel for that
participant's hand at the seated camera distance. The mapping thresholds
themselves are rig constants rather than per-participant values, so this step
adjusted seating position rather than the mapping, a point taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{preprint_fig3.png}
    \caption{Flowchart of the experimental session, $N=22$.}
    \label{fig:methodflow}
\end{figure}

Each condition then ran as a block. Participants took an untimed practice trial
with no data recorded, completed five fragile and five deformable trials, and
answered the post-condition questionnaire immediately afterwards. The board was
reflashed between conditions, which gave a natural rest break. After the third
condition, participants answered two closing forced-choice questions and were
invited to give free-form comments.

Two operator actions punctuate each trial. At the end of a fragile trial the
experimenter records whether the object survived, and at the end of every trial
the recording is explicitly kept or discarded before the log file is closed.
The survival judgement is written into the trial filename, which is what the
analysis pipeline reads.

\section{Instruments}
\label{sec:instruments}

\subsection{Quantitative Logging}
\label{subsec:logging}

Per-tick logs are as described in Section~\ref{sec:logging-arch}, written at
\SI{15}{\hertz} for the duration of each recorded trial. Fragile-object
outcome, survived or broke, was scored by the experimenter immediately after
each trial and recorded alongside the log.

\subsection{Post-Condition Questionnaire}
\label{subsec:questionnaire}

After each condition, participants rated six statements on a five-point Likert
scale from 1, strongly disagree, to 5, strongly agree. The same six statements
were used in all three conditions, covering ease of manipulation, contact
detection, confidence in the grasp, perceived force, mental effort, and
physical effort. The full wording is reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:questionnaire}.

Two forced-choice questions closed the session. The first asked which condition
gave the best sense of contact and grasp state, and the second asked which
condition the participant preferred overall. Both offered all three conditions
as options, labelled in plain language as no feedback, vibration motor and pin
actuator. A free-text field invited any further comments.

\section{Analysis Procedures}
\label{sec:analysis-procedures}

Per-trial metrics (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}) are reduced to one value per
participant per condition per object class using the median across the five
repeated trials, so that a single unusually careless grasp cannot dominate a
participant's contribution. Results are reported as medians and interquartile
ranges rather than means and standard deviations, for robustness to the
high-force outliers the study is designed to detect.

Four tests are used, each matched to the shape of the outcome.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Across the three conditions}, the Friedman test, which is the
          non-parametric repeated-measures analogue of one-way ANOVA. Where the
          omnibus test is significant, pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
          follow, with Holm correction across the three condition pairs.
    \item \textbf{Between LRA and EM alone}, a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
          test, which isolates the actuator-type variable because everything
          upstream of the actuator is identical in the two conditions.
    \item \textbf{Fragile-object survival}, which is a paired binary outcome.
          Cochran's Q tests across all three conditions and an exact binomial
          McNemar test compares LRA against EM.
    \item \textbf{Equivalence between LRA and EM}, tested with paired two
          one-sided tests. The equivalence margin is set at half the pooled
          standard deviation of the metric, a conventional medium effect size,
          with a per-side significance level of 0.05. This test is reported
          because a failure to find a difference is not the same claim as a
          demonstrated equivalence, and only the second is a positive result.
\end{itemize}

All analyses are implemented in the accompanying \texttt{analysis} package,
which regenerates the tables and figures of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} from the
raw logs. CSV outputs referenced by filename in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are
written to \texttt{analysis/results/}.

\section{Derived Per-Trial Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Four metrics are computed from each trial log. All four are oriented so that
lower is better.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peak deformation volume} (a.u.), the maximum deformation
          volume reached during the trial, used as the primary grip-force
          proxy.
    \item \textbf{Excess deformation} (a.u.), the deformation volume
          accumulated after indentation depth has plateaued. The plateau is
          taken as the first sample at which depth reaches
          \SI{95}{\percent} of its trial maximum. Past that point the display
          has saturated under Equation~\eqref{eq:mapping} and further squeezing
          conveys nothing new.
    \item \textbf{Grip adjustments} (count), the number of squeeze and release
          reversals after the plateau, read as a sign of searching for an
          appropriate grip rather than holding steady.
    \item \textbf{Peak indentation depth} (\si{\milli\metre}), the maximum of
          the depth map, reported mainly as a check on the safety ceiling.
\end{itemize}

Each metric is first reduced to one value per participant per condition per
object class by taking the median across the five repeated trials, and the
tables below report the median and interquartile range of those per-participant
values across the sample. Because the force proxy was never calibrated in
newtons, the two sensors are combined by taking the larger of the two raw
proxies rather than by summing calibrated readings. The analysis package
supports both, and the uncalibrated path is the one used throughout this
chapter.

\section{LRA Versus EM}
\label{sec:lra-vs-em}

Because the two haptic conditions share an identical sensor, intensity
calculation and control mapping, a direct paired comparison between them
isolates the actuator as the only variable. No such comparison reaches
significance. On fragile objects the paired Wilcoxon tests give $p = 0.66$ for
peak deformation volume, $p = 0.49$ for excess deformation, $p = 0.23$ for grip
adjustments and $p = 0.55$ for peak indentation depth. Survival does not
separate them either, whether tested as a rate ($p = 0.69$) or with an exact
McNemar test on the binary outcome, where five participants survived more often
under the LRA and one under the EM ($p = 0.22$).

A failure to find a difference is not the same claim as a demonstrated
equivalence, and the two are worth separating explicitly. Paired two one-sided
tests were run against a margin of half the pooled standard deviation of each
metric. Only excess deformation on fragile objects passes, and only narrowly,
with $p = 0.046$. Peak deformation volume ($p = 0.066$), peak indentation depth
($p = 0.13$) and grip adjustments ($p = 0.31$) all fail. For those three
metrics the study is therefore inconclusive in both directions, since it can
neither detect a difference nor rule one out at the chosen margin. The honest
summary is that this study does not distinguish the two actuators, and for most
metrics it is not powered to declare them equivalent either.

\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The results separate into a strong effect and a weak one.

The strong effect is the presence of tactile feedback. For fragile objects,
both haptic conditions raised survival by 16 to 19 percentage points over
visual-only feedback, and that improvement is statistically reliable at the
per-participant rate level for both actuators. Grip adjustments after the
plateau also fell reliably, though for the EM condition only. The mechanism is
visible in the time-series traces. Under visual-only feedback the operator has
no signal indicating when the useful part of the grasp is complete and
continues to squeeze after contact is fully established, whereas the haptic
conditions supply exactly that signal.

The metric that measures this most directly, excess deformation, shows the
largest descriptive change of any metric in the study, falling roughly ninefold
under the LRA. It is worth being careful about what that supports. The omnibus
test on excess deformation does not reach significance at this sample size, so
the reduction is a strong descriptive pattern rather than a confirmed effect.
What carries the argument is that the benefit appears in survival, which is the
outcome that actually matters and which is significant, rather than in the
force proxy alone.

The weak effect is actuator type. The LRA produced the lower excess deformation
and was the more frequently preferred condition, while the EM produced fewer
grip adjustments. No comparison between them reaches significance on any
metric, and no questionnaire item distinguishes them. Equivalence testing does
not rescue the null either, since only one metric passes at the chosen margin
and the rest are inconclusive in both directions. This study establishes that
feedback helps, does not establish which actuator helps more, and is not
powered to declare the two interchangeable.

The absence of any effect on deformable objects is not a failure of the system
but a property of the task. A foam cube imposes no penalty for over-squeezing,
so a channel whose value lies in discouraging over-squeezing has nothing to act
on. This asymmetry is itself informative, since it suggests the benefit of
tactile feedback in teleoperation is concentrated in tasks with a failure
threshold rather than distributed across manipulation generally.

\section{Why the Two Actuators Did Not Separate}
\label{sec:why-no-separation}

Section~\ref{sec:existing-approaches} set out a mechanistic reason to expect
the two actuators to differ. An LRA vibrates near \SI{200}{\hertz} and so works
through the fast FA-II channel, whereas a pin that presses into the fingerpad
and stays there delivers sustained pressure, which only the slowly adapting
receptors register. That prediction was not borne out, and three features of
the implementation explain why.

The first is that the sustained channel was never used. To render a graded
intensity rather than a single on-off contact, the driver pulses the pin
instead of holding it extended (Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive}). Both conditions
therefore delivered a gated waveform, differing in temporal granularity rather
than in kind, and the one property that would have separated them mechanically
was left on the table.

The second is that the intended rendering contrast was narrowed from the other
side as well. The LRA is driven by envelope modulation of a fixed-amplitude
carrier rather than by true amplitude modulation, because the microcontroller
cannot generate the phase-locked antiphase pair that amplitude modulation would
need (Section~\ref{subsec:lra-drive}). Both actuators are therefore switched
rather than modulated, which is closer to a comparison of two pulse trains than
of a continuous signal against a discrete one.

The third is that the display saturates well before the operator stops
squeezing. The mapping of Equation~\eqref{eq:mapping} reaches full intensity at
\SI{1.0}{\milli\metre} of indentation, and both actuators carry intensity
coarsely, since roughly a fifth of the range is needed before two levels can be
told apart \cite{choi2013}. Whatever differences the two actuators have in
rendering fidelity are compressed into a signal with few distinguishable
levels, which limits how much the choice of actuator could matter even in
principle.

A participant comment states the untested hypothesis more directly than the
data can. One wrote that the vibration motor felt more reactive for detecting
contact, while the pin might be better if the goal were to hold and apply
sustained pressure. That is close to the division of labour between fast and
slow receptors described in Section~\ref{sec:role-of-touch}, and it suggests
the comparison this thesis set out to make is still worth making with a driver
that holds the pin rather than pulsing it.

\section{The Gap Between What Participants Felt and What They Did}
\label{sec:subjective-objective}

The clearest divergence in the study is between the subjective and objective
results. On the questionnaire, four of six items separate the haptic conditions
from visual-only feedback, with perceived force rising from a median of 1 out
of 5 to 3.5 under the LRA. In the forced-choice questions, 21 of 22
participants preferred some form of haptic feedback and 17 named the LRA. Yet
no objective metric distinguishes the LRA from the EM at all.

Two readings are available and the data do not decide between them. The first
is that the preference tracks something real that the four metrics do not
capture, such as how promptly the display responds or how confident the
operator feels while working. The second is that the preference tracks
something incidental to the haptic channel.

The free-text comments favour the second reading more than the first. Three
participants independently reported that finger tracking degraded while the EM
modules were worn, describing fingertip positions that fluctuated even when
held still. This is corroborated in the implementation rather than only in
self-report, since the hand-tracking confidence thresholds had to be lowered
below their defaults because the actuator body occludes the two landmarks the
pinch mapping depends on, and at stock settings the tracker lost the hand
entirely during the EM condition (Section~\ref{sec:handtracking}). The EM
housing is also the bulkier of the two and sits on the fingertip, where it
interferes most. A participant who found the EM condition harder to control
would plausibly report preferring the LRA without the haptic rendering
differing at all.

If that reading is right, the preference result measures the ergonomics of the
wearable rather than the merits of the transduction method, and it reinforces
rather than weakens the conclusion that this study cannot rank the two
actuators.

\section{Comparison with Prior Work}
\label{sec:comparison}

The survival result is consistent with the closest published precedent. Becker
et al.\ reported a \SI{48}{\percent} reduction in deformation of a plasticine
ball when vibrotactile feedback derived from a GelSight sensor was enabled, in
a preliminary study with seven participants \cite{becker2024}. The present
study finds an effect in the same direction on a fragile rather than a
deformable object, at a larger sample, and with survival rather than
deformation as the outcome. Notably, the deformable-object result here is null,
which is worth setting against that precedent, since a plasticine ball records
its deformation permanently whereas the foam cube used here recovers between
trials and therefore carries no penalty for over-squeezing.

The direction also matches results from adjacent settings. Gong et al.\
measured significantly lower contact forces when scaled tool vibrations were
returned to thirty operators during telerobotic assembly \cite{gong2024}, and
Chai et al.\ found that encoding grip force as skin stimulation improved
fragile-object handling on a virtual eggs task in prosthetics \cite{chai2022}.
Coppola et al.\ reported reduced effort and physical demand with vibrotactile
feedback in a hand-tracked teleoperation setup close in form to this one,
though with a simulated robot hand \cite{coppola2022}. Across all of these, the
benefit of adding a cutaneous channel is reasonably well established, and the
present study adds a case where that benefit shows up in whether the object
survives.

What none of these studies answer, and what this one also fails to answer, is
whether the actuator itself matters. Each pairs one actuator with one pipeline.
This thesis held the pipeline fixed and varied only the actuator, which is the
comparison the field lacks, and found no difference. That is a useful negative
result only to the extent that the comparison was actually made, and
Section~\ref{sec:why-no-separation} argues that the implementation narrowed it
considerably.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several features of the study bound how strongly these conclusions can be
stated.

\paragraph{Condition order.} Conditions were presented in a fixed order rather
than counterbalanced across participants. Practice effects across the session
and fatigue toward its end are therefore confounded with condition, and a
difference attributed to an actuator may in part reflect its position in the
session.

\paragraph{The two actuators differ in more than one way.} They differ in
transduction mechanism, but also in where they sit. The EM pin must press on
the fingerpad to be felt and so occupies the distal phalanx, while the LRA is
strapped over the middle phalanx (Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive}). The two
therefore stimulate different skin, and the bulkier EM housing also degraded
hand tracking while it was worn, as Section~\ref{sec:subjective-objective}
describes. A preference for one actuator may reflect smoother control, or a
more sensitive site, rather than a better sensation.

\paragraph{Rendering narrows the intended contrast.} Both conditions set
intensity by gating a waveform on and off, for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:why-no-separation}, so they differ chiefly in temporal
granularity. A dedicated resonant-driver IC capable of amplitude modulation on
the LRA side, and a latch-and-hold drive on the EM side, would sharpen the
contrast the study was built to test.

\paragraph{The EM actuator's motion may be orientation-dependent.} The
single-direction behaviour reported in Section~\ref{subsec:em-drive} was
verified with the hand in its normal wearing orientation, palm and fingertips
facing down. A follow-up check with the hand inverted (palm up) found no
perceptible movement in either direction, which falls below the same script's
own threshold for a conclusive result and was not repeated, so it should be
read as suggestive rather than confirmed. It is nonetheless consistent with
gravity, not magnetic force alone, contributing to the motion felt in the
normal orientation, which would make the actuator's already limited behaviour
further dependent on how the hand is held. The study itself is unaffected,
since participants wore the device in a single, consistent, palm-down
orientation throughout, but the mechanism should not be assumed to generalise
to other mountings without checking.

\paragraph{Off-resonance carrier.} The LRA carrier was set empirically to
\SI{200}{\hertz} rather than tuned to the measured resonance of the specific
actuators. Since an LRA is efficient only in a narrow band around resonance,
the achievable amplitude range, and therefore the perceptual dynamic range of
the intensity signal, may have been compressed.

\paragraph{Non-haptic cues were available.} Participants could see and hear the
remote scene. One reported noticing that the egg was about to fail from visible
cracks or faint sounds rather than from the display. The visual-only condition
is therefore a realistic baseline rather than a sensory-deprivation control,
and the measured benefit of haptic feedback is the benefit on top of whatever
those incidental cues already provided.

\paragraph{The pinch mapping is not per-participant.} The two pixel thresholds
of Equation~\eqref{eq:pinchmap} are rig constants rather than per-participant
values (Section~\ref{sec:handtracking}). Hand size and seated camera distance
therefore vary across participants without compensation, so the same physical
pinch does not command the same gripper position for everyone. Because the
design is within-subjects this does not bias the comparison between conditions,
but it adds between-participant variance that reduces power.

\paragraph{Statistical power.} Several descriptively large differences do not
reach significance, and the equivalence tests of
Section~\ref{sec:lra-vs-em} are inconclusive for three of four metrics. At
$N=22$ the study can detect the survival effect but is underpowered for the
smaller actuator-versus-actuator comparison it was designed around.

\paragraph{Uncalibrated force.} Grip force is inferred from deformation volume
in arbitrary units, not measured in newtons, and indentation depth rests on a
single-ball calibration never checked against an independent gauge, so its
millimetre scale carries unquantified error.

\paragraph{Tracking and latency.} Hand tracking is 2D and uncalibrated, and the
end-to-end sensor-to-actuator latency still requires the bench measurement
described in Section~\ref{sec:latency}. Several participants reported
perceiving a delay, which makes that measurement worth having rather than
merely tidy.

\section{Energy-Based Modelling of the Loop}
\label{sec:phs}

At an abstract level the system is an interconnection of energy-exchanging
subsystems, namely the gripper-object contact, the sensing and computation
pipeline, and the actuator-skin interface. Teleoperation is commonly analysed
this way, treating each subsystem as a two-port element and using passivity of
the resulting energy flow as a sufficient condition for stability, an approach
that extends to port-based models of the whole loop \cite{niemeyer2016}. A full
port-Hamiltonian treatment, with the contact, the sensor's deformation
measurement, and the EM actuator's stored magnetic energy each modelled as a
port, lies beyond the scope of this thesis. It would, however, bear directly on
the question of Section~\ref{sec:why-no-separation}, since the difference
between holding a pin against the skin and pulsing it is a difference in how
energy is delivered, and an energetic account would predict what the present
comparison could not measure.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This thesis presented an end-to-end tactile-haptic teleoperation system that
integrates vision-based tactile sensing into a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive gripper,
commands the gripper from the operator's own pinch aperture, and renders the
sensed contact deformation on a wearable actuator array through either linear
resonant actuators or single-direction pulsed electromagnetic actuators, with
an identical sensing and intensity-mapping pipeline upstream of the driver in
both cases.
That last property is what the system was built for, since it allows the
actuator to be exchanged while everything before it is held fixed.

Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} posed two questions, and the study answers
them asymmetrically.

The first asked whether tactile feedback improves grip-force control and grasp
outcomes for fragile and deformable objects. For fragile objects it does.
Object survival rose from \SI{62}{\percent} under visual-only feedback to
\SI{81}{\percent} with the LRA and \SI{78}{\percent} with the EM, and that
improvement is statistically reliable for both actuators. Grip adjustments made
after the grasp was complete fell reliably under the EM. Excess deformation,
the force applied after the display had saturated, fell by close to an order of
magnitude, although that reduction does not reach significance at this sample
size. For deformable objects the answer is no, and that null result is
informative rather than disappointing, since an object with no failure
threshold gives a channel whose value lies in discouraging over-squeezing
nothing to act on. Participants agreed with the objective picture, with 21 of
22 preferring some form of haptic feedback to none.

The second asked whether the type of actuator matters when the sensing and
intensity mapping are held identical. On this evidence it does not, but the
finding is weaker than it looks. No objective metric and no questionnaire item
separates the two actuators, yet 17 of 22 participants named the LRA as their
overall preference. Equivalence testing fails to establish that the two are
interchangeable for three of four metrics, so the result is a genuine absence
of evidence rather than evidence of absence. Section~\ref{sec:why-no-separation}
argues that the implementation narrowed the intended contrast, because the pin
was pulsed rather than held and the LRA envelope was gated rather than
amplitude-modulated, leaving both actuators delivering switched waveforms that
differ mainly in temporal granularity.

The overall conclusion is therefore that the presence of tactile feedback
matters and, on this evidence and at this sample size, the choice between these
two actuators does not demonstrably matter. The second half of that sentence
should be read as a statement about this experiment rather than about the
actuators.

Four pieces of work follow directly, in rough order of how much they would
change the answer.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sharpen the actuator contrast.} Drive the pin in a
          latch-and-hold mode so that the sustained-pressure channel is
          actually exercised, and drive the LRA with a resonance-tuned
          amplitude-modulated stage. Without this the comparison remains
          between two pulse trains.
    \item \textbf{Remove the confounds.} Counterbalance condition order, match
          actuator placement on the hand, and calibrate the force proxy in
          newtons so that grip force is reported in physical units rather than
          arbitrary ones.
    \item \textbf{Measure the loop.} The end-to-end sensor-to-actuator latency
          is still unmeasured, and several participants reported perceiving a
          delay. A bench measurement would establish whether the perceived lag
          is real and, if so, where in the chain it accumulates.
    \item \textbf{Use the rest of the signal.} The tactile sensor produces a
          full depth map, of which this study renders a single scalar per
          finger, and the driver already carries five channels of which two
          were used. Rendering the spatial structure of the contact patch
          across the array is the most promising direction for recovering more
          of the information the sensor already measures.
\end{enumerate}

The broader point is that the sensing side of teleoperated grasping is now
considerably richer than the display side. A compact vision-based sensor
resolves contact geometry to tens of micrometres, while the wearable display
that must convey it carries perhaps a handful of distinguishable levels per
finger. Closing that gap, rather than improving either end alone, is where the
next gain in teleoperated handling of delicate objects is likely to come from.

\chapter{Questionnaire Items}
\label{app:questionnaire}

The questionnaire was administered as a single online form. Section~A was
completed once at the start of the session. Section~B was completed three
times, once immediately after each condition block, with identical wording each
time. Section~C was completed once at the end of the session.

\section*{A. Background and screening}

\begin{enumerate}[label=A\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
    \item Participant ID
    \item Name
    \item Age
    \item Gender
    \item Height (cm)
    \item Dominant hand
    \item Prior experience with teleoperation
    \item Prior experience with VR/gaming controllers
    \item Prior experience operating robotic arms/manipulators
    \item Do you have any visual, auditory, or tactile sensory impairments
          relevant to this study?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{B. Post-condition items}

Rated on a five-point scale, where 1 is strongly disagree and 5 is strongly
agree.

\begin{enumerate}[label=B\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
    \item It was easy to manipulate objects using the teleoperation system.
    \item I could clearly tell when the robot hand made contact with an object.
    \item I felt confident that the object was securely grasped in the robot
          hand.
    \item I could perceive how much force I was applying to the object.
    \item Mental effort required for this task was low.
    \item Physical effort required for this task was low.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{C. Closing items}

The three response options were labelled in plain language, as No Feedback,
Vibration Motor, and Pin Actuator, corresponding to the visual-only, LRA, and
EM conditions respectively.

\begin{enumerate}[label=C\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
    \item Which condition gave you the best sense of contact/grasp state?
    \item Which condition did you prefer overall?
    \item Any additional comments or suggestions? (optional)
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Firmware and Driver Details}
\label{app:firmware}

This appendix collects the hardware and protocol details behind
Section~\ref{sec:driver}. The timing parameters below are those compiled into
the firmware and are reproduced here so that the drive schemes can be
replicated without reading the source.

\section*{Serial protocol}

The host sends one ASCII line per haptic tick at \SI{115200}{baud}, carrying
the two channel intensities to four decimal places and separated by a comma.
The firmware clamps each value to $[0,1]$ and discards a malformed line rather
than raising, so a corrupted byte cannot stop the drive loop. A watchdog zeroes
both channels if no line arrives for \SI{200}{\milli\second}.
