  Across Haptic Actuator Types for Fragile and Deformable Objects

  \section{Introduction}

  When we pick up an egg, our fingers let us know how hard we are squeezing, so we stop
  before it cracks. In \emph{teleoperation}, where a user controls a robot remotely, that sense
  is missing, and the user can only guess whether the grip is too loose to hold the object or
  firm enough to break it.

  The common solution is to have the user wear a \emph{haptic actuator}, a device that reproduces that
  sense via vibrations or forces applied to the skin (Patel et al., 2025), but most research focuses on
  the effect of a single type of actuator on performance (Thomas et al., 2019). We ask both: does touch
  feedback help grasp specific objects, and does the actuator type matter?

  \section{System}

  Figs.~\ref{fig:hardware} and \ref{fig:signalflow} depict the system setup. A two-finger gripper,
  equipped with gel-based touch sensors (Lin et al., 2023), is controlled in real time by the
  user's pinching motion, using their thumb and index finger.

  Two actuator types are compared in this study. The first is a \emph{linear resonant actuator (LRA)},
  a small motor that vibrates along one axis, like the kind used in smartwatches (Pacchierotti et al., 2017);
  the second is an \emph{electromagnetic actuator (EM)} adapted from Vechev et al. (2019).

  \section{Method}

  Fig.~\ref{fig:methodflow} lays out the experiment workflow for all $N=22$ participants.

  \begin{tightcenter}
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{preprint_fig3.eps}
    \captionof{figure}{Flowchart of the experiment.}
    \label{fig:methodflow}
  \end{tightcenter}

  Each touch sensor reports two quantities: \emph{deformation volume}, an uncalibrated proxy for
  grip force in arbitrary units (a.u.), and \emph{dent depth} in millimetres, which is mapped to a
  0-to-1 haptic intensity scale. Once depth plateaus, further squeezing changes nothing the user feels; we
  call that wasted effort \emph{excess deformation}. \emph{Grip adjustments} count how often a
  participant alternated between squeezing and easing off after the plateau, a sign of searching
  for the right grip rather than holding steady.

  \begin{tightcenter}
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{preprint_fig4.eps}
    \captionof{figure}{Object classes. (a) A hollow plastic egg. (b) The same egg with
      separated halves. (c) A foam cube.}
    \label{fig:objects}
  \end{tightcenter}

  Participants grasped two classes of object, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:objects}. \emph{Fragile} objects
  break suddenly when squeezed too hard, whereas \emph{deformable} ones change shape gradually and
  never break at all.

  \textbf{Objective results} (Tab.~\ref{tab:quant}, Fig.~\ref{fig:results}): For fragile objects, touch feedback reliably
  cut excess deformation with the LRA (roughly ninefold) and grip adjustments with the EM, while survival rose from
  62\% with vision only to 81\% with the LRA and 78\% with the EM. Deformable objects showed no reliable change in
  dents or breakage.

  \textbf{Subjective results} (Fig.~\ref{fig:likert}): Participants significantly preferred touch feedback over vision
  only, especially for feeling grip force and grasp confidence. The LRA was the clear favorite, but no single survey
  question reliably told the two actuators apart.

  \section{Discussion}

  The main limitations are the fixed trial order, which may have introduced practice or
  fatigue effects; the two actuators differing in several ways at once, including how they physically
  work, where they are placed on the hand, and how well hand tracking worked, so any preference may
  reflect smoother control rather than a better sensation; 2D and uncalibrated hand tracking; grip
  force inferred from deformation volume rather than measured in newtons; dent depth resting on a
  single-ball sensor calibration that was never checked against an independent gauge, so its
  millimetre scale carries unquantified error; and an end-to-end delay still needing a bench
  measurement.
  Future work can explore using spatial feedback information instead of a single intensity value per
  finger.

  \section{Conclusion}

  Touch feedback made grasping fragile objects safer, while a larger, better-controlled
  study is needed to tell the two actuators apart.
