\begin{center}
  {\bfseries Title: Tactile-Feedback Teleoperation: Grip Force and Grasping Performance \\
    Across Haptic Actuator Types for Fragile and Deformable Objects}\\[4pt]
\end{center}

\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{multicols}{2}

  \section{Introduction}

  Teleoperated grasping is normally performed with vision alone. Without contact
  information at the fingertips, the feedforward grip-force control humans rely
  on is severed [1], and the operator must infer from images whether a grasp is
  too light to hold or hard enough to crush. Wearable haptic interfaces address
  this, but nearly all fix the actuator hardware in advance and drive it from a
  single-point force sensor or motor-current proxy, leaving the actuator itself
  untested as a variable [2].

  This study builds a teleoperation loop in which a vision-based tactile sensor
  on the gripper drives two mechanically distinct wearable actuator arrays
  through an identical sensing and control pipeline, and evaluates both against a
  visual-only baseline ($N=20$, within-subjects) on fragile and deformable
  objects: does sensor-driven feedback improve grip-force regulation and grasp
  outcome relative to vision alone, and do the two actuator technologies differ?

  \section{System}

  The loop connects a host PC, a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive gripper, and an ESP32-C6
  microcontroller worn on the operator's hand. Each control tick
  ($\approx$30~Hz), the host reads gripper state over Modbus RTU, tracks the
  operator's thumb--index pinch distance with MediaPipe to command aperture, and
  reads two 9DTact vision-based tactile sensors [3] on the gripper's fingertips.
  From each height map it computes a deformation volume (the grip-force proxy)
  and a maximum indentation depth; depth is normalised against a per-object-class
  saturation point (2.0~mm fragile, 0.6~mm deformable), clipped to $[0,1]$, and
  streamed per finger to the ESP32-C6 over USB serial. A 200~ms watchdog zeroes
  the actuators if the stream stalls, and a runtime cutoff blocks further closing
  once either sensor reaches 1.0~mm of indentation, protecting the fixture.

  Two arrays are driven from that same stream. The LRA array uses linear resonant
  actuators at the thumb and index proximal joints, driven by a bipolar
  $\approx$200~Hz carrier whose active envelope fraction within a 50~ms window
  scales with intensity. The TacTiles array uses bistable electromagnetic pin
  actuators [4] at the thumb and index fingertips, driven by latching H-bridge
  pulses in bursts whose inter-burst gap shortens with intensity, holding the
  switching rate under the thermal limit ($\approx$120~switches/min). Both render
  a continuous, intensity-scaled sensation; the conditions differ in actuator
  technology \emph{and} mounting site, a consequence of each actuator's form
  factor rather than an independent variable.

  \section{Method}

  Twenty participants each completed a within-subjects session with the three
  feedback conditions (visual-only, LRA, TacTiles) in counterbalanced order, on
  two object classes: fragile objects, which fail abruptly under excess force,
  and deformable objects, which yield progressively. Five trials per condition per class gives 600 logged
  trials; participants saw the workspace only through a fixed camera. Trials were logged at $\approx$30~Hz, and each yielded peak
  force proxy, peak depth, time to first contact, force overshoot (force rise
  after depth has plateaued), approach rate, post-plateau force reversals, dwell
  time above 90\% of peak force, and, for fragile objects, survival. Per-trial
  values were reduced to a per-participant median.
  Conditions were compared with a Friedman test and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank
  tests with Holm correction; LRA versus TacTiles was also tested for equivalence
  by TOST (0.5~SD margin). Seventeen participants completed a 5-point Likert
  questionnaire after each condition.

  \section{Results}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{preprint_results.png}

      condition; (b) per-participant post-plateau force overshoot (log, $+1$).}
    \label{fig:results}

  On fragile objects, haptic feedback measurably changed how participants closed
  the gripper (Table~\ref{tab:quant}). Peak force proxy differed across
  conditions ($p=.022$), LRA reducing the median by roughly 40\% against
  visual-only. The clearest effect was force overshoot: squeezing that continued
  after the tactile signal had stopped changing fell from a median of 1770 (a.u.)
  under visual-only to 170 under LRA ($p=.025$ corrected). Post-plateau force
  reversals, a proxy for hunting for the right force, also dropped ($p=.0039$
  omnibus; visual-only vs.\ TacTiles $p=.030$). Fragile objects survived 63\% of
  visual-only trials against 80\% (LRA) and 77\% (TacTiles), significant on
  per-participant rates ($p=.015$; visual-only vs.\ LRA $p=.050$) though not
  under a stricter binary test (Cochran's $Q$, $p=.135$). No deformable-object
  comparison survived correction.

  No metric separated LRA from TacTiles after correction, and TOST established
  equivalence for only two of fourteen metric--object pairs: the actuators are not
  distinguished at this sample size, not shown to be equivalent.

  \section{Discussion and Conclusion}

  Sensor-driven feedback improved fragile-object handling on both behavioural and
  subjective measures, through a specific mechanism: participants stopped
  squeezing once the tactile signal plateaued, and stopped hunting for the right
  force afterwards. The effect is confined to the fragile class, consistent with
  deformable objects indenting the gel too little to drive a strong signal, and
  having no abrupt failure to avoid.

  The two actuator types were not distinguishable, and that comparison is
  confounded: they differ in mounting site as well as technology, both render
  intensity by gating a drive waveform rather than by amplitude modulation, and
  the LRA carrier was set empirically rather than tuned to resonance. The
  preference for LRA (12/17) is thus a preference for technology and placement
  together. Other limitations: the force proxy is an uncalibrated deformation
  volume rather than newtons, peak depth was censored by the safety cutoff,
  end-to-end latency still needs a bench measurement, and the questionnaire's
  scale endpoints were unlabelled.

  Future work will calibrate the force proxy against a load cell, drive the LRA
  through a dedicated resonant-actuator IC for true amplitude modulation, mount
  both actuators at the same site to separate placement from technology, and
  extend the per-finger scalar to a spatial mapping.
