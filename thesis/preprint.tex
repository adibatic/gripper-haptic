    Across Haptic Actuator Types for Fragile and Deformable Objects}\\[2pt]

  \section{Introduction}

  Teleoperated grasping is normally vision-only. Without fingertip contact
  information, feedforward grip-force control is disrupted [1], leaving
  operators to infer from visual cues alone whether a grasp is too weak to
  secure an object or too forceful for a fragile one. Wearable haptic
  interfaces provide tactile feedback, but most studies fix the actuator design
  and rely on a single-point force sensor or motor-current proxy [2].

  This study compares two mechanically distinct wearable actuators driven by
  the same gripper-mounted vision-based tactile sensor, evaluated against a
  visual-only baseline ($N=22$, within-subjects) on fragile and deformable
  objects. We ask whether sensor-driven tactile feedback improves grip-force
  regulation and grasp outcome, and whether actuator technology influences
  performance.

  \section{System}

  The teleoperation system connects a host PC, a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive gripper,
  and a hand-worn ESP32-C6 microcontroller. Each control tick ($\approx$30~Hz),
  the host reads gripper state over Modbus RTU, tracks the operator's
  thumb--index pinch distance with MediaPipe to command aperture, and reads
  two 9DTact tactile sensors [3] on the gripper's fingertips. From each
  height map it computes a deformation volume (the grip-force proxy) and a
  maximum indentation depth, normalised against a per-object-class
  saturation point (2.0~mm fragile, 0.6~mm deformable), clipped to $[0,1]$,
  and streamed per finger over USB serial. A runtime cutoff blocks closing past
  1.0 mm of indentation.

  Two actuator configurations are driven from that same stream. The LRA configuration uses
  linear resonant actuators at the thumb and index proximal joints, driven by a bipolar
  $\approx$200~Hz carrier whose active envelope fraction within a 50~ms window
  scales with intensity. The electromagnet (EM) configuration adapts bistable electromagnetic
  actuators [4] at the thumb and index fingertips, removing the original design's contact pin
  for a single neodymium magnet to support continuous vibration, driven by latching H-bridge
  pulses in bursts whose inter-burst gap shortens with intensity, holding the switching rate
  under the thermal limit ($\approx$120~switches/min). Both render continuous, intensity-scaled
  sensation; the two differ in actuator technology and mounting site as a consequence of form
  factor, not by design.

  \section{Method}

  Twenty-two participants each completed a within-subjects session with three
  conditions (visual-only, LRA, EM) on two object classes, fragile (fails
  abruptly under excess force) and deformable (yields progressively).
  Visual-only ran first for nearly all participants, with LRA/EM order varied
  after it; order was not fully counterbalanced. Five trials per condition
  per class gives 660 trials, logged at $\approx$30~Hz, each yielding
  peak force proxy, peak depth, time to first contact, force overshoot (force
  rise after depth has plateaued), approach rate, post-plateau force
  reversals, dwell time above 90\% of peak force, and, for fragile objects,
  survival, reduced to a per-participant median. Conditions were compared
  with a Friedman test and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm
  correction; LRA versus EM was also tested for equivalence by TOST (0.5~SD
  margin). All participants completed a 5-point Likert questionnaire after
  each condition, plus two forced-choice preference items.

  On fragile objects, haptic feedback changed how participants closed
  the gripper (Table~\ref{tab:quant}). Force overshoot fell from a median of
  1530 (a.u.) under visual-only to 170 (LRA) and 260 (EM), corrected
  $p=.019$ and $p=.061$, though the omnibus test, underpowered at three
  conditions, fell short of significance ($p=.079$). Post-plateau force
  reversals (hunting for the right force) fell from 7 to 5 (LRA) and 2 (EM),
  the one metric significant at both levels ($p=.0048$ omnibus; vs.\ EM
  $p=.021$). Peak force proxy fell $\approx$40\% under LRA, short of
  significance ($p=.083$ corrected). Fragile objects survived 62\% of
  visual-only trials (pooled) against 81\% (LRA) and 78\% (EM);
  per-participant medians (Table~\ref{tab:quant}) differ likewise
  ($p=.0036$ omnibus; $p=.018$ and $p=.037$ against LRA and EM), though not
  under a stricter binary test (Cochran's $Q$, $p=.097$). No deformable-object
  comparison survived correction (smallest corrected $p=.11$).

  Subjectively, both haptic conditions were rated well above baseline
  ($n=22$; Fig.~\ref{fig:likert}a): force perception shifted most (median
  $1\rightarrow3.5$ LRA, $1\rightarrow3$ EM, both $p=.0003$), grasp
  confidence $2\rightarrow4$ and contact detection $2.5\rightarrow4$ (all
  $p\le.0015$), ease of manipulation $3\rightarrow4$ ($p\le.043$); the two
  effort items moved similarly but were not significant. No item
  separated LRA from EM (all $p\ge.13$). In the forced choice
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:likert}b), 17 of 22 participants preferred LRA overall
  ($\chi^2=19.7$, $p=.0001$) and 16 rated it best for sensing contact and grasp
  state ($\chi^2=17.8$, $p=.0001$).

  \section{Discussion and Conclusion}

  Sensor-driven feedback improved fragile-object handling on behavioural and
  subjective measures alike, through a specific mechanism. Participants
  stopped squeezing once the tactile signal plateaued, and stopped hunting
  for the right force afterwards. The effect is confined to the fragile
  class, consistent with deformable objects indenting the gel too little to
  drive a strong signal and having no abrupt failure to avoid.

  No behavioural metric separated LRA from EM after correction, and TOST
  established equivalence for only three of fourteen metric--object pairs,
  so the actuators are undistinguished at this sample size, not shown
  equivalent. That comparison is confounded by mounting site, technology,
  and tracking quality together. EM's fingertip placement covers the
  MediaPipe landmarks used for aperture control more than LRA's proximal
  mounting, degrading detection confidence (thresholds were lowered to
  compensate), so the subjective LRA preference (17/22) may partly reflect
  this rather than sensation alone.

  The largest limitation is order. Visual-only ran first for nearly all
  participants rather than in counterbalanced sequence, confounding condition
  with practice on the pinch-tracking control and gripper response, so
  visual-only-vs-haptic comparisons may partly reflect familiarisation.
  LRA-vs-EM comparisons, whose order was varied, are less exposed. Other
  limitations include uncalibrated 2D pixel-space pinch tracking (no depth
  or orientation compensation), an uncalibrated force proxy (deformation
  volume, not newtons), peak depth censored by the safety cutoff,
  end-to-end latency still needing a bench measurement, and unlabelled
  questionnaire scale endpoints. Future work will re-run with counterbalanced
  order, calibrate the proxy against a load cell, drive the LRA via a
  dedicated resonant-actuator IC, co-locate both actuators, and extend the
  per-finger scalar to a spatial mapping.
