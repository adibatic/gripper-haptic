  {\bfseries Title: Tactile-Feedback Teleoperation: Grip Force and Grasping Performance \\
    Across Haptic Actuator Types for Fragile and Deformable Objects}\\[4pt]
\end{center}
\vspace{0.5\baselineskip}   % 2 lines

  \section{Introduction}

  Teleoperated grasping is normally performed with vision alone. Without
  fingertip contact information, the feedforward grip-force control humans rely
  on is severed [1], and the operator must infer from images whether a grasp is
  too light to hold or hard enough to crush. Wearable haptic interfaces address
  this, but nearly all fix the actuator in advance and drive it from a single-point
  force sensor or motor-current proxy, leaving the actuator untested as a variable [2].

  This study builds a teleoperation loop in which a gripper-mounted vision-based
  tactile sensor drives two mechanically distinct wearable actuator configurations
  through an identical pipeline, evaluated against a visual-only baseline
  ($N=22$, within-subjects) on fragile and deformable objects: does sensor-driven
  feedback improve grip-force regulation and grasp outcome, and do the two
  actuator technologies differ?

  \section{System}

  The loop connects a host PC, a Robotiq 2F-85 adaptive gripper, and an ESP32-C6
  microcontroller worn on the operator's hand. Each control tick
  ($\approx$30~Hz), the host reads gripper state over Modbus RTU, tracks the
  operator's thumb--index pinch distance with MediaPipe to command aperture, and
  reads two 9DTact tactile sensors [3] on the gripper's fingertips.
  From each height map it computes a deformation volume (the grip-force proxy)
  and a maximum indentation depth; depth is normalised against a per-object-class
  saturation point (2.0~mm fragile, 0.6~mm deformable), clipped to $[0,1]$, and streamed
  per finger over USB serial. A 200~ms watchdog zeroes
  the actuators if the stream stalls, and a runtime cutoff blocks closing past
  1.0~mm of indentation, protecting the fixture.

  Two actuator configurations are driven from that same stream. The LRA configuration uses
  linear resonant actuators at the thumb and index proximal joints, driven by a bipolar
  $\approx$200~Hz carrier whose active envelope fraction within a 50~ms window
  scales with intensity. The electromagnet (EM) configuration adapts bistable electromagnetic
  actuators [4] at the thumb and index fingertips. Unlike the original design, this version
  removes the contact pin in favor of just a single neodymium magnet to support continuous
  vibration. It is driven by latching H-bridge pulses in bursts whose inter-burst gap shortens
  with intensity, holding the switching rate under the thermal limit ($\approx$120~switches/min).
  Both render a continuous, intensity-scaled sensation; the conditions differ in actuator
  technology and mounting site, a consequence of form factor rather than an independent variable.

  \section{Method}

  Twenty-two participants each completed a within-subjects session with the three
  conditions (visual-only, LRA, EM) in counterbalanced order, on two object
  classes: fragile, which fail abruptly under excess force, and deformable,
  which yield progressively. Five trials per condition per class gives 660
  logged trials. Trials were logged at $\approx$30~Hz, each yielding peak force proxy,
  peak depth, time to first contact, force overshoot (force rise after depth has plateaued),
  approach rate, post-plateau force reversals, dwell time above 90\% of peak force, and,
  for fragile objects, survival, reduced to a per-participant median.
  Conditions were compared with a Friedman test and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank
  tests with Holm correction; LRA versus EM was also tested for equivalence
  by TOST (0.5~SD margin). All participants completed a 5-point Likert
  questionnaire after each condition, plus two forced-choice preference items.

  On fragile objects, haptic feedback changed how participants closed
  the gripper (Table~\ref{tab:quant}). Force overshoot (squeezing after the
  tactile signal had plateaued) fell from a median of 1530 (a.u.) under
  visual-only to 170 (LRA) and 260 (EM), corrected $p=.019$ and $p=.061$,
  though the omnibus test --- underpowered at three conditions --- fell
  short of significance ($p=.079$). Post-plateau force reversals, a proxy
  for hunting for the right force, fell from 7 to 5 (LRA) and 2 (EM), the
  one metric significant at both levels ($p=.0048$ omnibus; vs.\ EM
  $p=.021$). Peak force proxy fell $\approx$40\% under LRA, short of
  significance ($p=.083$ corrected). Fragile objects survived 62\% of
  visual-only trials (pooled) against 81\% (LRA) and 78\% (EM);
  per-participant medians (Table~\ref{tab:quant}) differ likewise
  ($p=.0036$ omnibus; $p=.018$ and $p=.037$ against LRA and EM), though not
  under a stricter binary test (Cochran's $Q$, $p=.097$). No deformable-object
  comparison survived correction (smallest corrected $p=.11$).

  Subjectively, both haptic conditions were rated well above baseline
  ($n=22$; Fig.~\ref{fig:likert}a): force perception shifted most (median
  $1\rightarrow3.5$ LRA, $1\rightarrow3$ EM, both $p=.0003$), grasp
  confidence $2\rightarrow4$ and contact detection $2.5\rightarrow4$ (all
  $p\le.0015$), ease of manipulation $3\rightarrow4$ ($p\le.043$); the two
  effort items moved the same way but did not survive correction. No item
  separated LRA from EM (all $p\ge.13$). In the forced choice
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:likert}b), 17 of 22 participants preferred LRA overall
  ($\chi^2=19.7$, $p=.0001$) and 16 rated it best for sensing contact and grasp
  state ($\chi^2=17.8$, $p=.0001$).

  \section{Discussion and Conclusion}

  Sensor-driven feedback improved fragile-object handling on both behavioural and
  subjective measures, through a specific mechanism: participants stopped
  squeezing once the tactile signal plateaued, and stopped hunting for the right
  force afterwards. The effect is confined to the fragile class, consistent with
  deformable objects indenting the gel too little to drive a strong signal and
  having no abrupt failure to avoid.

  No behavioural metric separated LRA from EM after correction, and TOST
  established equivalence for only three of fourteen metric--object pairs: the
  actuators are undistinguished at this sample size, not shown equivalent. That
  comparison is confounded: they differ in mounting site as well as
  technology, both gate a drive waveform rather than modulating amplitude, and
  the LRA carrier was set empirically rather than tuned to resonance. So the
  subjective LRA preference (17/22) is one of technology and placement together. Other limitations: the force proxy is an uncalibrated deformation
  volume rather than newtons, peak depth was censored by the safety cutoff,
  end-to-end latency still needs a bench measurement, and the questionnaire's
  scale endpoints were unlabelled. Future work will calibrate the proxy against a
  load cell, drive the LRA through a dedicated resonant-actuator IC for true
  amplitude modulation, mount both actuators at the same site, and extend the
  per-finger scalar to a spatial mapping.
